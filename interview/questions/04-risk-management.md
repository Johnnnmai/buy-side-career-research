# Risk Management and Portfolio Construction

Risk management separates professional investors from amateurs. In hedge fund interviews, demonstrating that you think about risk at both the position level and the portfolio level signals maturity that interviewers value highly. A brilliant stock picker who cannot manage risk will blow up a fund. A solid stock picker with excellent risk management will compound capital over decades.

I have watched candidates nail their stock pitch and then completely fall apart when asked how they would construct a portfolio. The pivot from "here is a great idea" to "here is how this idea fits into a portfolio of 30 positions with correlated risks" is where interviewers separate analysts from future portfolio managers.

The questions in this section test whether you understand that investing is a portfolio exercise, not a stock-picking contest. The best individual idea in the world is worthless if it blows up the portfolio because of concentration, correlation, or leverage risk that you failed to identify.

Risk management is also where experience matters most. You can study stock picking from books. You can learn modeling from courses. But risk management is something you internalize through living through drawdowns and making mistakes with real capital. If you have not managed real money, study the mistakes of others. Read about the blowups -- Long-Term Capital Management, Archegos, the London Whale. Understand what went wrong at a portfolio construction level, not just the headline narrative.


## What interviewers evaluate

Position-level risk management. Can you articulate the specific risks for an individual position? What would cause you to cut the position? Do you use stop losses? How do you think about risk/reward asymmetry? A position with 50.0% upside and 40.0% downside has very different risk characteristics than one with 30.0% upside and 10.0% downside, even if the expected values are similar.

Portfolio-level risk awareness. Do you understand how individual positions interact? A portfolio of 10 "uncorrelated" long positions can become highly correlated during a market selloff. Can you identify hidden factor exposures and correlation risks? If your top five longs are all high-beta technology stocks, you do not have five independent positions -- you have one large tech/growth bet expressed through five names.

Drawdown thinking. How do you think about maximum drawdown? What happens to your portfolio in a 2008-style credit crisis? In a 2020-style pandemic shock? In a 2022-style rate rotation? Interviewers want to see that you stress test your thinking beyond the base case. The question is not whether drawdowns will happen -- they will -- but whether you have thought about their magnitude and your response.

Practical experience with losses. If you have managed real capital, interviewers want to hear how you handled positions that went against you. If you have not managed capital, they want to hear how you would handle losses. There are no investors who have never lost money -- the question is how you respond. Do you freeze? Do you double down? Do you cut mechanically? Each response reveals something about your temperament.

Understanding of risk metrics. Can you discuss gross exposure, net exposure, beta-adjusted exposure, factor tilts, sector concentration, and single-name concentration? These are the operational language of hedge fund risk management. If you cannot discuss these concepts fluently, you are not prepared for the conversation.

## Portfolio construction frameworks

Top-down versus bottom-up allocation. Some funds start with macro views and allocate capital across sectors before selecting individual names. Others build portfolios purely from the bottom up, selecting their best ideas regardless of sector allocation. Most funds use a hybrid approach -- bottom-up idea generation with top-down risk constraints. Know the tradeoffs of each. Pure bottom-up can lead to unintended sector concentration. Pure top-down can force you into positions where you lack conviction.

Gross and net exposure management. Gross exposure (longs plus shorts as a percentage of capital) determines how much total market risk you are taking. Net exposure (longs minus shorts) determines your directional bias. A typical long/short equity fund runs 150.0% to 200.0% gross and 30.0% to 60.0% net. Understanding why these ranges exist and when to adjust them is critical. Higher gross means more opportunity but also more risk. Higher net means more directional exposure. A fund running 200.0% gross and 20.0% net is making a bet that alpha will exceed the cost of leverage, with minimal directional exposure. A fund running 120.0% gross and 80.0% net is effectively a levered long fund.

Position sizing framework. I think about position sizing along three dimensions. First, conviction level -- how differentiated is your view and how much work have you done? Second, risk/reward asymmetry -- what is the upside/downside ratio? Third, liquidity -- how many days of volume does your position represent? A high-conviction idea with 3:1 upside/downside and good liquidity might warrant a 4.0% to 5.0% position. A lower-conviction idea with 2:1 risk/reward might get 1.0% to 2.0%. A position that requires 10 days of volume to exit should be smaller than one you can exit in 2 days, regardless of conviction.

Factor exposure management. Every position carries factor exposures -- market beta, sector, size, value, momentum, quality. A portfolio can look diversified on a name basis but be heavily exposed to a single factor. If you own 15 stocks across 8 sectors but they all have betas above 1.3, you have a concentrated beta exposure that will hurt in a risk-off environment. Understanding your portfolio's factor tilts and managing them intentionally is what separates sophisticated portfolio construction from naive diversification.

Sector concentration limits. Most funds have guidelines for maximum sector concentration, typically 20.0% to 30.0% of gross exposure. This prevents a portfolio from becoming a disguised sector bet. Know these conventions and understand the reasoning behind them. Even within sector limits, be aware of sub-sector concentration -- owning five semiconductor stocks that all move with chip cycle demand is effectively one bet.

Correlation analysis. The key question in portfolio construction is not "is each position a good idea?" but "how do these positions behave together?" Two positions that are individually attractive but highly correlated effectively function as a single, larger position. Building a portfolio with genuinely uncorrelated return streams requires active effort and continuous monitoring. Correlations are not static -- they tend to increase during market stress, which is exactly when you need diversification most.


## Risk management tools

Stop losses. A mechanical rule to cut a position at a predetermined loss level, typically 10.0% to 20.0% from entry. Stop losses prevent small losses from becoming large losses but can also force you out of positions at the worst time. Know the tradeoffs. Some PMs use hard stops (automatically executed), others use soft stops (trigger a review process). The right approach depends on the fund's style and the position's characteristics.

Hedging with options. Buying puts on individual positions or index puts for portfolio-level protection. Understand the cost of hedging and the tradeoff between protection and drag on returns. A portfolio that hedges every risk will not generate returns. The key is identifying which risks are worth paying to hedge and which are part of the risk you are compensated for taking. Index puts protect against market risk but not idiosyncratic risk. Single-name puts are more targeted but more expensive.

Pair trades. Going long one stock and short a correlated stock to isolate the specific driver you want exposure to. For example, going long one retailer and short another to bet on relative market share without taking directional retail risk. Pair trades reduce your net and market exposure while allowing you to express stock-specific views. The risk is that the correlation between the pair breaks down.

Scenario analysis. Modeling how your portfolio behaves under different macro scenarios -- recession, rate shock, sector rotation, liquidity crisis, geopolitical shock. This is a planning exercise, not a prediction exercise. The goal is to identify vulnerabilities before they materialize. If a recession scenario shows your portfolio losing 30.0%, you know you need to either reduce exposure or add hedges before the recession arrives.

Dynamic exposure management. Adjusting gross and net exposure based on market conditions and opportunity set. When you have high conviction and the opportunity set is attractive, run higher gross. When uncertainty is elevated and the opportunity set is thin, reduce gross. This dynamic approach requires discipline -- the temptation is to run high gross when markets are calm and reduce when they get volatile, which is the opposite of what you should do.


## Drawdown management

Every hedge fund will experience drawdowns. How you think about managing through them reveals your maturity as an investor.

The first question in a drawdown is whether your positions are down because your theses are wrong or because the market is repricing risk broadly. If the market is selling everything and your fundamental views have not changed, maintaining or adding to positions may be the right response. If the market is sending you new information that challenges your thesis, cutting positions is prudent.

Know your fund's drawdown limits. Most investors and fund-of-funds have implicit or explicit drawdown tolerances. If a fund loses 10.0% from peak, investors start calling. At 15.0%, some begin redeeming. At 20.0%, the fund may face an existential crisis. Understanding these dynamics helps you calibrate your risk taking. Running 200.0% gross exposure when you are already down 8.0% from peak is reckless, regardless of how good your ideas are.

Have a pre-defined playbook. Decide in advance what actions you will take at different drawdown levels. At minus 5.0%, reduce gross by 15.0%. At minus 10.0%, cut maximum position size from 5.0% to 3.0%. At minus 15.0%, shift the portfolio to higher-quality, more liquid names. Having this playbook prevents you from making emotional decisions during stressful periods.

Communication matters. During a drawdown, investors want to hear that you understand what happened, what you are doing about it, and why your process will recover. "We had concentrated exposure to a factor rotation that we did not anticipate. We have reduced gross from 180.0% to 140.0% and shifted toward more idiosyncratic positions" is a better answer than "the market was irrational."


## Common mistakes

Ignoring correlation. Building a portfolio of 20 positions that all have the same factor exposure is not diversification. If all your longs are high-beta growth stocks, a factor rotation will hit your entire book simultaneously. True diversification requires holding positions whose returns are driven by different factors and different catalysts.

Confusing hedging with diversification. A hedge is a specific, intentional offset to a known risk. Diversification is holding multiple uncorrelated positions. Buying puts is hedging. Owning stocks in different sectors is diversification. They are different tools for different purposes. Knowing when to use each is a sign of sophistication.

No clear risk limits. Saying "I would manage risk carefully" without specifying position size limits, sector concentration limits, or stop loss levels signals that you have not thought about risk operationally. Be specific. "Maximum single-name concentration of 5.0%, maximum sector concentration of 25.0%, soft stop loss at 15.0% from entry with a thesis review" is a much better answer.

Overcomplicating risk management. Some candidates try to impress by discussing VaR, Greeks, and complex risk models. If you are interviewing for a fundamental equity role, you need to demonstrate practical risk management, not quantitative risk modeling. Focus on position sizing, correlation awareness, and disciplined loss management. Save the quant discussion for quant fund interviews.

Refusing to take risk. Risk management is not risk elimination. The goal of a hedge fund is to take intelligent, well-compensated risks. A candidate who wants to hedge everything signals that they are not comfortable with the fundamental nature of the business. The question is not whether to take risk but which risks are worth taking and how to size them appropriately.

Anchoring to cost basis. Many candidates fall into the trap of thinking about risk relative to their entry price rather than relative to current value. The question is always "given what I know today, is this the best use of this capital?" not "am I up or down from where I bought it." The market does not care about your cost basis.

Not thinking about tail risk. Most risk management conversations focus on normal market conditions -- what happens if your stock drops 10.0% or a sector rotates. But hedge fund blowups happen during tail events -- a credit crisis, a pandemic, a currency devaluation. If your risk framework does not include stress testing for events that happen once every 10 years, it is incomplete. Interviewers at experienced funds will probe whether you think about fat tails and convexity.

Treating the short book as a mirror of the long book. Some candidates construct their short book as simply the inverse of their long book -- they short bad companies while going long good ones. But short positions have asymmetric risk because the maximum gain is 100.0% while the potential loss is unlimited. Position sizing, stop losses, and catalyst timelines all need to be more conservative on the short side. A short that goes against you by 50.0% costs the same as a long that goes against you by 50.0%, but the behavioral dynamics are different because a short going against you means the stock is going up, which often triggers covering pressure and further squeezes.


## Key risk metrics to know

Be fluent with these risk metrics before your interview. You do not need to calculate them on the spot, but you should be able to discuss them intelligently.

Sharpe ratio. Measures risk-adjusted return -- the excess return per unit of volatility. A Sharpe ratio above 1.0 is generally considered good for a long/short fund. Above 2.0 is exceptional. Know what drives Sharpe -- it is as much about reducing volatility as it is about increasing returns.

Sortino ratio. Similar to Sharpe but only penalizes downside volatility. Many investors prefer Sortino because upside volatility is desirable. A fund that makes 20.0% with occasional large up-months will have a lower Sharpe than its Sortino suggests, because the Sharpe penalizes the upside volatility.

Maximum drawdown. The largest peak-to-trough decline in the fund's history. Most investors care more about maximum drawdown than about average return. A fund that returns 15.0% per year with a 10.0% maximum drawdown is generally preferred over one that returns 20.0% per year with a 35.0% maximum drawdown, because the second fund is more likely to trigger redemptions at the worst time.

Beta. The sensitivity of the portfolio to market movements. A beta of 0.3 means the portfolio captures roughly 30.0% of market moves. Long/short equity funds typically target a beta between 0.2 and 0.5. A beta near zero means the portfolio is market-neutral. Know the difference between raw beta and beta-adjusted net exposure.

Gross and net exposure. Gross equals longs plus shorts divided by NAV. Net equals longs minus shorts divided by NAV. A fund running 160.0% gross and 40.0% net has 100.0% in longs and 60.0% in shorts. These are the most basic and most frequently discussed risk metrics in hedge fund conversations.
