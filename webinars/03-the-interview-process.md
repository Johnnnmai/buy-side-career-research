# Webinar 3: The Hedge Fund Interview Process

Date: March 2, 2026
Host: Alexey Grigorev ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/agrigorev))
Format: Live session with Q&A and live mock pitch critique, approximately 120 minutes


## Overview

The hedge fund interview process is nothing like banking or consulting recruiting. There is no standardized structure, no case book to memorize, and no "right answer" format. Each fund designs its own process based on what its PM values. This session deconstructs the interview process across fund types, shares the patterns I have identified from 200+ interview reports, and includes a live mock pitch critique with real-time feedback.


## Topics Covered

Interview structure analysis: I break down the typical stages across three fund archetypes. Multi-manager platforms (Citadel, Millennium, Balyasny) typically run 4-6 rounds including a recruiter screen, a sector-specific technical round, a stock pitch presentation, a modeling test, a PM conversation, and a culture fit round. Single-manager funds often compress this into 2-3 rounds with heavier emphasis on the pitch and personal chemistry. Quant funds replace the stock pitch with coding assessments and probability questions.

Stock pitch best practices: the pitch is where interviews are won or lost. I cover the structure that works: start with the thesis in one sentence, establish the variant perception (what the market is missing), walk through the key drivers, present your valuation with a clear margin of safety, define the catalyst timeline, and articulate what would make you wrong. The biggest mistake candidates make is presenting research without a view. "I looked at this company and here is what I found" is not a pitch. "This stock is mispriced because X, and the market will realize it when Y happens within Z months" is a pitch.

Modeling test preparation: take-home modeling tests typically give you 3-7 days to build a full model and investment memo for a company you have never covered. In-office tests give you 3-5 hours. I walk through my preparation framework: practice building a three-statement model from a 10-K in under 4 hours. Know your shortcuts. Have a template you can adapt quickly. The model is not the point; the investment conclusion you draw from it is.

Behavioral questions for buy-side: buy-side behavioral questions are different from banking. Nobody cares about "tell me about a time you worked on a team." They care about "tell me about a time you were wrong about an investment," "what is the most controversial opinion you hold about a market or sector," and "describe a situation where you changed your mind on a position." Prepare 3-4 stories that show intellectual honesty, conviction, and the ability to update your views.

Live mock pitch critique: I invite two audience members to deliver a 5-minute stock pitch and provide real-time feedback on structure, conviction, variant perception, and common pitfalls. This is the most popular segment of the series based on attendee feedback.


## Key Takeaways

Your pitch is your calling card. It represents your research process, your analytical rigor, and your ability to form conviction. Spend more time on your pitch than on anything else in your prep.

The modeling test is a filter, not a differentiator. You need to pass it, but nobody gets hired because their model was beautiful. You get hired because your investment conclusion was thoughtful and your model supported it.

Behavioral questions on the buy side are really testing for intellectual character. Can you hold a view under pressure? Can you admit when you are wrong? Do you have genuine curiosity about markets? These are not soft skills. They are core competencies for an analyst.

Every interview is a two-way evaluation. You are also assessing whether this PM, this team, and this fund structure will allow you to do your best work. Ask questions that show you understand the business: "How is risk allocated across the book?" "What is the typical holding period?" "How do you handle drawdowns?" These questions signal sophistication.


## Common Mistakes

Pitching a consensus name. If your pitch is Apple, Amazon, or any stock that every sell-side analyst covers, you need a genuinely differentiated angle. Otherwise, pick a less-followed name where your edge is more visible.

Over-building the model. The PM reviewing your take-home case study does not want to see 47 tabs in your Excel file. They want to see a clean model that leads to a clear conclusion. Complexity for its own sake is a negative signal.

Not preparing for "what would make you wrong." Every experienced interviewer asks this. If you cannot articulate the bear case on your own pitch, it signals you have not done the work or you are not being honest about the risks.

Treating the PM conversation as a formality. The PM round is the most important round. This is where they assess whether they want to sit next to you for 12 hours a day. Be genuine, be curious, and demonstrate that you understand what the job actually involves.


## Q&A Highlights

Question: How many stock pitches should I have prepared?

Have two polished pitches ready: one long and one short. The long should be in a sector you know well. The short demonstrates a different skill set and shows you can think about downside. Have a third pitch loosely prepared in case they ask for something in a specific sector.

Question: How do I handle a modeling test for an industry I have never covered?

This is the whole point of the test. They want to see your process, not your industry expertise. Start with the 10-K. Read the MD&A section first to understand what management thinks drives the business. Build your revenue model from the key operating metrics they disclose. Do not try to be an industry expert in 48 hours. Be an honest analyst who can build a reasonable model from first principles.

Question: What do I do if the interviewer disagrees with my pitch?

This is a test, and it is a good sign. They want to see how you handle pushback. Do not cave immediately, and do not dig in stubbornly. Acknowledge the counterargument, explain why you weighed the evidence differently, and be willing to say "that is a fair point, and here is what would change my view." The worst response is to abandon your thesis the moment someone pushes back.
