# Webinar 4: Investment Case Studies

Date: March 9, 2026
Host: Alexey Grigorev ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/agrigorev))
Format: Live session with Q&A and case study walkthrough, approximately 120 minutes


## Overview

Take-home case studies are the most decisive round in hedge fund recruiting. The pitch tests your thinking. The case study tests your process, your speed, and your ability to deliver a complete investment recommendation under real constraints. This session analyzes real case study prompts, walks through my approach from blank page to finished memo, and covers the mistakes that sink otherwise strong candidates.


## Topics Covered

Analyzing real take-home case studies: I walk through three anonymized case study prompts from real hedge fund interviews. One is a 48-hour take-home from a long/short equity fund asking for a full investment memo on a mid-cap industrial company. One is a 72-hour case from an event-driven fund asking for analysis of a pending merger. One is a 5-hour in-office case from a multi-manager platform asking for a quick thesis and valuation on a consumer company. For each, I explain what the fund is really testing and how to allocate your time.

Building investment memos under time pressure: the biggest challenge of a case study is not the analysis. It is time management. I share my exact framework for a 48-hour case: hours 1-4, read the 10-K, listen to the most recent earnings call, and form an initial hypothesis. Hours 5-10, build the model. Hours 11-14, do targeted research on the 2-3 questions that will determine whether your hypothesis holds. Hours 15-18, write the memo. Hours 19-20, review and refine. Sleep in between. Do not pull an all-nighter. Tired analysts make bad decisions, and the quality of your thinking matters more than the volume of your output.

Common mistakes in case deliverables: I have reviewed dozens of case study submissions and the failure modes are consistent. The most common mistake is writing a research report instead of making a recommendation. The fund gave you a case study because they want to see you take a position. "This is an interesting company with both positives and negatives" is a failing grade. "I recommend a long position with a 12-month price target of X because of reasons A, B, and C, and I would exit if D happens" is what they want.

Other common mistakes: spending too much time on the model and not enough on the thesis. Ignoring the downside case entirely. Failing to define a catalyst or timeline. Submitting a 30-page document when 8-10 pages would have been tighter and more effective. Using complicated financial jargon when simple language would convey the idea better.

Live case study walkthrough: I take a real company (disclosed during the session) and build a mini investment memo in real time, narrating my thought process at each step. This is not a polished presentation. It is a messy, real-time demonstration of how I actually think through a new investment idea, including the dead ends and the moments of uncertainty.


## Key Takeaways

The case study tests three things: can you form a view, can you support it with evidence, and can you communicate it clearly. Technical modeling ability is necessary but not sufficient.

Time allocation is a skill. Most candidates spend 60-70% of their time on the model and 10-15% on the memo. I recommend the opposite ratio for the memo and thesis development, with the model serving as support for your argument rather than the centerpiece of it.

The bear case is not optional. Every strong memo includes a section titled something like "risks and what would change my mind." This is not a formality. It demonstrates that you have stress-tested your own thesis, which is exactly what a PM wants to see from an analyst.

Simplicity wins. The best case study submissions I have seen were 8-12 pages long, used clean formatting, had a one-page executive summary at the front, and made their recommendation clear in the first paragraph. The worst submissions were 40-page research reports with no clear point of view.


## Case Study Structure Template

This is the structure I recommend for a take-home case study:

Executive summary: one page. State your recommendation, your price target, your key thesis points, and the primary risks. The reader should understand your full argument without turning the page.

Company overview: half a page. Do not waste space repeating what is in the 10-K. Focus on the 2-3 aspects of the business that are relevant to your thesis.

Investment thesis: 2-3 pages. This is the core of your memo. Each thesis point should follow the pattern: here is what I believe, here is the evidence, here is why the market disagrees or has not priced this in.

Valuation: 1-2 pages. Show your work but keep it concise. A DCF summary, a comparable company table, and a sensitivity analysis on your key assumptions. Link your valuation to your thesis: if your thesis is right, the stock is worth X. If the bear case plays out, the stock is worth Y.

Risks and catalysts: 1 page. What would make you wrong, and what specific events could close the gap between the current price and your target.

Appendix: model outputs, additional data, supplementary analysis. This is for reference, not for reading.


## Q&A Highlights

Question: How do I handle a case study in an industry I know nothing about?

This is more common than you think, and it is often intentional. The fund wants to see how you learn a new industry quickly. Start with the company's own disclosures: the 10-K, the most recent investor presentation, and the last two earnings call transcripts. Then read 2-3 sell-side initiation reports if you can find them. Within 4-5 hours, you should have a working understanding of the industry drivers. You do not need to be an expert. You need to be a fast learner who can identify what matters.

Question: Should I build a full three-statement model or is a simpler approach acceptable?

It depends on the fund. If they explicitly ask for a model, build one. If they ask for an investment memo, a simpler valuation approach is fine as long as your assumptions are clear and defensible. I have seen candidates get hired with a well-reasoned comparable company analysis and no DCF at all. The model is in service of the thesis, not the other way around.

Question: How do I differentiate my case study from other candidates analyzing the same company?

Your variant perception is your differentiator. Everyone will have similar financial data. The question is what you see that others might not. This could come from a channel check (talking to customers, suppliers, or competitors), a proprietary data source, a non-obvious read on management quality, or a different view on the industry cycle. If your thesis could have been written by reading the sell-side consensus, it is not differentiated enough.

Question: Is it acceptable to recommend not investing?

Absolutely. "Pass" is a valid recommendation, and sometimes the right one. If you genuinely believe the stock is fairly valued and there is no clear edge, saying so with a well-reasoned explanation is stronger than forcing a thesis you do not believe. Just make sure your "pass" is backed by real analysis, not laziness.
