# Risk Management and Portfolio Construction

Risk management separates professional investors from amateurs. In hedge fund interviews, demonstrating that you think about risk at both the position level and the portfolio level signals maturity that interviewers value highly. A brilliant stock picker who cannot manage risk will blow up a fund. A solid stock picker with excellent risk management will compound capital over decades.

I have watched candidates nail their stock pitch and then completely fall apart when asked how they would construct a portfolio. The pivot from "here is a great idea" to "here is how this idea fits into a portfolio of 30 positions with correlated risks" is where interviewers separate analysts from future portfolio managers.


## What interviewers evaluate

Position-level risk management. Can you articulate the specific risks for an individual position? What would cause you to cut the position? Do you use stop losses? How do you think about risk/reward asymmetry?

Portfolio-level risk awareness. Do you understand how individual positions interact? A portfolio of 10 "uncorrelated" long positions can become highly correlated during a market selloff. Can you identify hidden factor exposures and correlation risks?

Drawdown thinking. How do you think about maximum drawdown? What happens to your portfolio in a 2008-style event? In a 2020-style event? In a sector rotation? Interviewers want to see that you stress test your thinking beyond the base case.

Practical experience with losses. If you have managed real capital, interviewers want to hear how you handled positions that went against you. If you have not managed capital, they want to hear how you would handle losses. There are no investors who have never lost money -- the question is how you respond.

Understanding of risk metrics. Can you discuss gross exposure, net exposure, beta-adjusted exposure, factor tilts, sector concentration, and single-name concentration? These are the operational language of hedge fund risk management.

## Portfolio construction frameworks

Top-down versus bottom-up allocation. Some funds start with macro views and allocate capital across sectors before selecting individual names. Others build portfolios purely from the bottom up, selecting their best ideas regardless of sector allocation. Most funds use a hybrid approach. Know the tradeoffs of each.

Gross and net exposure management. Gross exposure (longs plus shorts as a percentage of capital) determines how much market risk you are taking. Net exposure (longs minus shorts) determines your directional bias. A typical long/short equity fund runs 150.0% to 200.0% gross and 30.0% to 60.0% net. Understanding why these ranges exist and when to adjust them is critical.

Position sizing framework. I think about position sizing along three dimensions. First, conviction level -- how differentiated is your view and how much work have you done? Second, risk/reward asymmetry -- what is the upside/downside ratio? Third, liquidity -- how many days of volume does your position represent? A high-conviction idea with 3:1 upside/downside and good liquidity might warrant a 4.0% to 5.0% position. A lower-conviction idea with 2:1 risk/reward might get 1.0% to 2.0%.

Factor exposure management. Every position carries factor exposures -- market beta, sector, size, value, momentum, quality. A portfolio can look diversified on a name basis but be heavily exposed to a single factor. Understanding your portfolio's factor tilts and managing them intentionally is what separates sophisticated portfolio construction from naive diversification.

Sector concentration limits. Most funds have guidelines for maximum sector concentration, typically 20.0% to 30.0% of gross exposure. This prevents a portfolio from becoming a disguised sector bet. Know these conventions and understand the reasoning behind them.

Correlation analysis. The key question in portfolio construction is not "is each position a good idea?" but "how do these positions behave together?" Two positions that are individually attractive but highly correlated effectively function as a single, larger position. Building a portfolio with genuinely uncorrelated return streams requires active effort.


## Risk management tools

Stop losses. A mechanical rule to cut a position at a predetermined loss level, typically 10.0% to 20.0% from entry. Stop losses prevent small losses from becoming large losses but can also force you out of positions at the worst time. Know the tradeoffs.

Hedging with options. Buying puts on individual positions or index puts for portfolio-level protection. Understand the cost of hedging and the tradeoff between protection and drag on returns. A portfolio that hedges every risk will not generate returns.

Pair trades. Going long one stock and short a correlated stock to isolate the specific driver you want exposure to. For example, going long one retailer and short another to bet on relative market share without taking directional retail risk.

Scenario analysis. Modeling how your portfolio behaves under different macro scenarios -- recession, rate shock, sector rotation, liquidity crisis. This is a planning exercise, not a prediction exercise. The goal is to identify vulnerabilities before they materialize.


## Common mistakes

Ignoring correlation. Building a portfolio of 20 positions that all have the same factor exposure is not diversification. If all your longs are high-beta growth stocks, a factor rotation will hit your entire book simultaneously.

Confusing hedging with diversification. A hedge is a specific, intentional offset to a known risk. Diversification is holding multiple uncorrelated positions. Buying puts is hedging. Owning stocks in different sectors is diversification. They are different tools for different purposes.

No clear risk limits. Saying "I would manage risk carefully" without specifying position size limits, sector concentration limits, or stop loss levels signals that you have not thought about risk operationally.

Overcomplicating risk management. Some candidates try to impress by discussing VaR, Greeks, and complex risk models. If you are interviewing for a fundamental equity role, you need to demonstrate practical risk management, not quantitative risk modeling. Focus on position sizing, correlation awareness, and disciplined loss management.

Refusing to take risk. Risk management is not risk elimination. The goal of a hedge fund is to take intelligent, well-compensated risks. A candidate who wants to hedge everything signals that they are not comfortable with the fundamental nature of the business.
