# Investment Case Studies

Case studies are the most comprehensive test in hedge fund interviews. They combine everything -- financial modeling, investment judgment, written communication, and the ability to work under time pressure. A strong case study demonstrates that you can do the actual job of a hedge fund analyst from start to finish.

Unlike quick verbal questions where charm and articulation can carry you, case studies expose the depth of your analytical capability. You either know how to pull apart a 10-K, identify the key drivers, build a model, and form a differentiated investment thesis, or you do not. There is no way to bluff through a 3-hour modeling test.

I think of case studies as the most honest part of the interview process. They simulate the actual work you will do on the job more closely than any other interview format. A take-home memo is essentially a sample of what you would produce in your first week on the desk.


## What interviewers evaluate

End-to-end investment analysis. Can you start with a raw set of financial statements and arrive at a well-reasoned investment recommendation? This tests the full chain: reading financials, identifying what matters, building projections, valuing the business, assessing risks, and forming a view. Each link in the chain has to work. A great model with a weak thesis is just as problematic as a great thesis with a broken model.

Written communication. Hedge fund analysts write investment memos that portfolio managers use to make capital allocation decisions. Your case study deliverable is a sample of your professional writing. Is it clear? Is it structured? Does it lead with the conclusion? Can a PM read your memo in five minutes and understand your thesis? Many technically skilled candidates fail because their writing is disorganized or verbose. Concise, structured writing is a competitive advantage.

Analytical rigor under time pressure. Timed case studies test whether you can produce quality work under constraints. In the real job, you often need to form a preliminary view on a company within 24 to 48 hours. This is the pace the case study simulates. The question is not whether your analysis is perfect -- it is whether you can produce useful, directionally correct work under time pressure.

Prioritization. A 10-K for a large company can be 200 pages. You do not have time to model every line item in a 3-hour test. Interviewers want to see that you can quickly identify the two or three assumptions that drive the valuation and focus your time there. Spending 45 minutes modeling the depreciation schedule when the stock moves on revenue growth is a prioritization failure.

Independent thinking. The case study is your chance to show that you can form your own view without being guided by a sell-side report or a PM's pre-existing thesis. The best case study submissions contain a genuinely independent perspective on the business. If your analysis reaches the same conclusion as the Street consensus for the same reasons, you have not demonstrated independent thinking.


## Types of case studies

Take-home stock pitch memo (2 to 7 days). The most comprehensive case study format. You choose or are assigned a company, and you produce a full investment memo of 3 to 10 pages with a supporting financial model. This format allows you to demonstrate deep research, including channel checks, competitor analysis, and industry research beyond the company's filings.

The memo should include an executive summary with your recommendation and price target, a business overview, your investment thesis with two to three supporting pillars, variant perception, catalysts, a valuation section referencing your model, risks and mitigants, and a position sizing recommendation. The financial model should include a 3-statement model with at least three years of projections, a DCF or comparable company analysis, and sensitivity tables on key assumptions.

The best take-home memos go beyond the 10-K. Call former employees on LinkedIn. Talk to customers. Analyze industry trade data. Reference competitive dynamics that are not discussed in sell-side research. This primary research is what separates a great memo from a good one.

Timed modeling test (2 to 4 hours). You receive a company's 10-K, 10-Q, or a packet of financial information and have a fixed amount of time to build a model and present your findings. This format tests speed and prioritization. You will not have time to build a perfect model, so you need to decide what matters and focus there.

Some funds provide a clean data packet. Others give you the raw 10-K and expect you to extract the relevant data yourself. The raw 10-K version is harder and more realistic. Practice with both formats. In either case, allocate your time roughly as follows: 20.0% on reading and understanding the business, 50.0% on building the model, and 30.0% on forming your thesis and preparing your presentation.

Market event analysis (1 hour). You are given a breaking news event -- a merger announcement, an earnings surprise, a regulatory change, a macro shock -- and asked to analyze its investment implications in writing within one hour. This tests your ability to quickly assess the impact on affected securities and form a trading view.

For example: "Company X just announced it is acquiring Company Y for $5B in cash and stock. Analyze the deal. Is it good for X shareholders? Should you buy or sell X? What about Y?" This format tests whether you can think clearly and quickly about complex situations with incomplete information.

Portfolio construction exercise. You are given a set of constraints -- $100M AUM, maximum 15 positions, gross exposure limit of 200.0%, sector concentration limit of 25.0% -- and asked to construct a portfolio. You need to select positions, size them, explain your reasoning, and demonstrate that the portfolio is balanced from a factor and sector perspective.

This format is less common but increasingly used at multi-manager platforms. It tests portfolio-level thinking rather than individual stock-picking ability. You need to show that you can think about how positions interact, manage gross and net exposure, and construct a book that generates alpha while controlling risk.


## Sample case study prompts

"Write a 3 to 5 page investment memo on a company of your choice with a 12-month price target."

"Here is a company's 10-K. Build a model and present your investment thesis in 3 hours."

"Company X just announced an acquisition. Analyze the deal and give your view."

"Construct a 10-position portfolio with $100M AUM. Defend your selections and sizing."

"This company just missed earnings by 15.0%. The stock is down 12.0% after hours. Would you buy the dip or sell? Walk me through your analysis."

"Here are the financials for two companies in the same industry. Which would you rather own for the next three years and why?"

"Write a short thesis on a company you believe is overvalued. Include a price target and catalyst timeline."

"A private equity firm just announced a take-private of this public company at a 30.0% premium. Analyze whether the price is fair."

"This company is trading at a 40.0% discount to its closest peer. Explain why the discount exists and whether it is justified."

"Build an LBO model for this company assuming a purchase price of 10x EBITDA. What equity return can a sponsor achieve over 5 years?"

"The CEO of this company just resigned unexpectedly. Analyze the impact on the stock and recommend a trade."

"Here is a basket of 5 stocks. Rank them from most to least attractive and explain your framework."

"This company is converting from a hardware business model to a subscription model. Project the financial impact over the next 5 years and give your view on the stock."

"An activist investor just disclosed a 9.0% stake in this company. Analyze what changes they might push for and whether the stock is attractive."

"Two competitors in the same industry just announced a merger. Analyze the impact on the remaining competitors."


## How to structure a case study deliverable

Executive summary. Start with your recommendation, price target, and the upside/downside ratio. A PM should be able to read the first paragraph and understand your view. Do not bury the conclusion on page 4. "I recommend a long position in XYZ at $34 with a $52 base case price target (53.0% upside) and $28 downside ($6 risk), implying a 2.6:1 risk/reward."

Business overview. Two to three paragraphs covering what the company does, its competitive position, end market dynamics, and key financial characteristics. Keep this brief -- the evaluator already knows the company. Focus on the aspects of the business that are most relevant to your thesis.

Investment thesis. Two to three pillars that support your recommendation. Each pillar should be a specific, testable claim about the business. "Management is strong" is not a thesis pillar. "The new CEO's cost reduction program will expand EBITDA margins from 18.0% to 22.0% over two years based on benchmarking against peers who have completed similar programs" is a thesis pillar. Each pillar should be supported by data and analysis, not assertion.

Variant perception. What does the consensus believe and where is it wrong? Reference sell-side estimates, short interest, and the prevailing narrative. Explain specifically where each is incorrect. This section is what makes your memo a hedge fund memo rather than a sell-side initiation report.

Catalysts. Identify two to three events that will cause the market to reprice the stock. Be specific about timing and magnitude. "Q3 earnings will show revenue acceleration" is better than "the market will eventually recognize the value." If your thesis lacks near-term catalysts, acknowledge that and explain why you believe patience will be rewarded.

Valuation. Present your valuation analysis with a base case, bull case, and bear case. Show the methodology, key assumptions, and sensitivity to those assumptions. The valuation should tie directly to your thesis -- if your thesis is about margin expansion, your valuation should be most sensitive to margin assumptions. Include a sensitivity table showing how the price target changes with different assumptions.

Risks and mitigants. Address the top three risks to your thesis. For each, explain why you believe the risk is manageable. Be intellectually honest -- if a risk is genuinely concerning, say so and explain how you size the position to account for it. "I acknowledge that competitive entry is a real risk, which is why I would size this at 3.0% rather than 5.0% until I see the Q3 data confirming retention rates."

Appendix. Include your financial model output, comparable company analysis, and any supporting data tables. This is where you put the detailed work that supports your thesis without cluttering the main memo.


## Time management for timed case studies

The biggest mistake candidates make on timed case studies is poor time allocation. Here is a framework for a 3-hour modeling test.

First 30 minutes. Read the financials and identify the business model. Determine the two or three key drivers. Decide on your modeling approach. Do not touch the spreadsheet during this time -- thinking first saves time later.

Minutes 30 through 150. Build the model. Focus on the revenue build and the key margin drivers. Get the income statement right first, then build the cash flow statement, then the balance sheet. If time is short, a working income statement and cash flow statement with a price target is more valuable than a perfectly balanced but incomplete 3-statement model.

Last 30 minutes. Form your investment thesis. Calculate your valuation. Prepare your presentation or write your summary. This is where most candidates run out of time because they spent too long on the model. Budget this time explicitly and protect it.


## Common mistakes

Too much description, not enough analysis. Many candidates write case studies that read like equity research initiation reports -- pages of industry background and company description with a thin layer of analysis on top. The evaluator wants to see your analytical framework, not your ability to summarize a 10-K. Every paragraph should advance your thesis or address a risk.

No clear price target. Every case study must include a specific price target with the supporting math. "I think the stock is cheap" is not a conclusion. "I believe the stock is worth $62 in my base case, implying 35.0% upside from the current price of $46" is a conclusion. Show the math that gets you there.

Ignoring risks. A case study that presents only the bull case signals that you are not a rigorous analyst. The best case studies dedicate 15.0% to 20.0% of the content to risks and demonstrate that the risk/reward is still attractive after accounting for them. Ignoring risks does not make them go away -- it makes the evaluator question your judgment.

Poor time management on timed tests. Candidates who spend 90.0% of their time building a perfect model and then rush through the investment thesis in the last 10 minutes have their priorities backward. The thesis matters more than the model. A PM cares about your investment view and its supporting logic, not whether your depreciation schedule ties out to the penny.

Copying sell-side research. If your case study reads like a Goldman Sachs initiation report, the evaluator will notice. They have read every sell-side report on the company. Your case study needs to add something that the sell-side does not provide -- a genuinely independent perspective. If you reach the same conclusion as the Street for different reasons, that is acceptable. If you reach the same conclusion for the same reasons, you have not added value.

Sloppy presentation. Typos, inconsistent formatting, broken links in your model, and decimal errors all signal carelessness. Proofread your work. Check that your model balances. Format your memo cleanly. A sloppy case study tells the interviewer that your work product on the desk will also be sloppy.


## Preparation strategy

Build 3 to 5 practice case studies before your interviews. Choose companies across different sectors and business models. Time yourself. Get feedback from friends in the industry if possible. The gap between your first practice case study and your fifth will be enormous. Each repetition builds both speed and analytical instinct.

Read real hedge fund investment memos. Several funds publish their letters to investors, which contain condensed versions of their investment theses. Pershing Square, Greenlight Capital, and Third Point publish detailed letters that demonstrate how professional investors structure their analysis. Study the structure, level of detail, and analytical frameworks used by professional investors.

Practice speed modeling. Set a timer for two hours and build a 3-statement model from a 10-K you have never read before. Do this five times with different companies. The goal is not perfection but fluency -- you should be able to build a working model that captures the key drivers without getting bogged down in immaterial line items.

Develop a personal template. Create a standard structure for your investment memos that you can adapt to any company. Having a template means you spend your time on analysis, not on figuring out how to organize your thoughts. The template should include all the sections described above in the deliverable structure section.

Practice presenting under pressure. Many case studies end with a verbal presentation and Q&A. Practice presenting your analysis concisely and handling pushback. Record yourself presenting and listen back for clarity and confidence. The presentation is where you sell your thesis -- if you mumble through it or cannot defend your assumptions, the written work loses its impact.

Study the fund's style before the case study. If the fund is known for deep value investing, a growth stock pitch may not resonate. If the fund runs a market-neutral book, a directional macro trade will miss the mark. Align your case study approach with the fund's investment philosophy while still demonstrating independent thinking.


## What separates a good case study from a great one

The difference between a good case study and one that gets you hired usually comes down to three things.

Primary research. A good case study uses public filings and sell-side research. A great case study includes at least one data point from primary research -- a conversation with a former employee, a proprietary analysis of web traffic data, a survey of customers, or an on-the-ground observation that is not available in any published report. Primary research demonstrates that you know how to generate informational edge.

Intellectual honesty. A good case study presents a bullish or bearish view. A great case study acknowledges the strongest argument against your thesis and explains specifically why you believe your view will prevail despite that counterargument. This is not about hedging your language. It is about demonstrating that you have stress-tested your thesis against the best opposing view.

Actionable specificity. A good case study says the stock is undervalued. A great case study specifies the entry price, the target exit price, the position size, the hedging strategy, the catalyst timeline, and the conditions under which you would exit the position at a loss. This level of specificity demonstrates that you are thinking like a portfolio manager, not an academic.


## Case study checklist

Before submitting any case study deliverable, run through this checklist.

Does the executive summary clearly state your recommendation and price target in the first paragraph?

Is your variant perception explicitly stated -- what does the market believe, and why is it wrong?

Have you identified at least two specific catalysts with timelines?

Does your valuation include base, bull, and bear cases with the key assumptions for each?

Have you addressed the top three risks and explained why they are manageable?

Does your financial model link correctly? Do the three statements balance?

Have you included a sensitivity table showing how your price target changes with different assumptions?

Is the memo under 5 pages (or whatever the specified length limit is)?

Have you proofread for typos, formatting inconsistencies, and decimal errors?

Would a PM reading this for the first time be able to understand your thesis in under 5 minutes?
