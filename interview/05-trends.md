# Interview Trends (2026)


## Market Data

The global hedge fund industry manages approximately $4.9 trillion in assets under management as of Q1 2026, up from $4.5 trillion at the end of 2024. Net inflows have been positive for seven consecutive quarters, driven largely by institutional allocators increasing alternative investment targets.

Multi-manager platforms continue aggressive expansion. Citadel added approximately 400 investment professionals in 2025. Millennium now operates over 330 portfolio manager teams globally. Point72 expanded its systematic equity division by 35.0% year-over-year. Balyasny opened offices in Abu Dhabi and Singapore to access regional talent.

This expansion translates directly into hiring volume. Multi-manager platforms collectively posted 2,800+ investment roles in the 12 months ending March 2026, up 22.0% from the prior year.

Compensation at a glance:

Junior analyst (0-2 years, typically post-banking): base compensation $150K-$200K, total compensation $250K-$400K in a good year. Base has increased 12.0% since 2023 due to competition with technology firms.

Mid-level analyst (3-5 years): base $200K-$300K, total compensation $400K-$700K. The variance is driven almost entirely by P&L contribution.

Senior analyst (6-10 years): base $250K-$400K, total compensation $500K-$1.5M+. At this level, a meaningful portion of compensation is deferred and tied to fund performance.

Portfolio manager: base $300K-$500K, total compensation is uncapped and primarily a function of P&L. Top-performing PMs at multi-manager platforms earn $5M-$50M+ annually. The median PM at a large platform earns $1.5M-$3M all-in.

These numbers reflect a competitive market. Funds that were previously rigid on compensation structures have started offering guaranteed first-year bonuses, sign-on packages, and accelerated promotion timelines to attract talent from competing funds and from technology companies.


## Hiring Trends Shaping Interviews

Alternative data literacy is now a baseline expectation. In our dataset, 47.3% of job postings mention alternative data (credit card data, web traffic, satellite imagery, app download data) as a required or preferred skill. Three years ago, this figure was under 20.0%. Interviewers increasingly ask candidates how they would use non-traditional data sources to gain an edge.

The types of alternative data questions appearing in interviews:

"How would you use credit card transaction data to forecast a retailer's same-store sales before earnings?" "If I gave you six months of app download data for a SaaS company, what would you look for?" "How do you evaluate the reliability and latency of a new alternative data vendor?" These questions test whether you can turn raw data into investment insight, not just whether you know the data exists.

Python and SQL have crossed from "nice to have" to "expected." 52.1% of analyst-level postings now list Python or SQL as a required skill, up from 31.0% in 2023. This does not mean you need to be a software engineer. It means you should be able to pull data from a database, clean a dataset, and run a basic regression without relying on someone else.

In interviews, this shows up as practical questions: "Walk me through how you would scrape and analyze customer review data for a consumer company" or "If I gave you a CSV of 10,000 transactions, what would your workflow look like to identify trends?" Candidates who answer with conceptual frameworks but cannot describe actual code or tools they have used are increasingly at a disadvantage.

AI and machine learning integration is creating new role types. "Fundamental analyst with quantitative overlay" is a job title that barely existed three years ago. Funds want analysts who can use large language models to process earnings transcripts at scale, run sentiment analysis on filings, and build systematic screens that complement their fundamental process. This is reshaping interview questions: expect to be asked how you would use AI tools in your research workflow.

Some specific AI-related interview questions that have surfaced in 2025-2026: "How do you use AI in your current research process, and where do you not trust it?" "If you could build any AI tool for fundamental investing, what would it do?" "What are the risks of over-relying on LLMs for investment research?" The best answers are grounded in actual usage, not theoretical possibilities.

The talent pipeline is shifting. Historically, the path to the buy side ran through investment banking (2 years) and then a move to a hedge fund. This pipeline still exists but is no longer the dominant one. Funds now recruit directly from management consulting, technology companies, equity research, and even graduate programs. 28.6% of analyst hires at multi-manager platforms in 2025 came from non-banking backgrounds, up from 18.0% in 2021.

This has practical implications for interview preparation. Candidates from non-banking backgrounds may face additional scrutiny on financial modeling skills but can differentiate through unique analytical perspectives. A former management consultant might bring superior framework thinking. A former technologist might bring data engineering skills that pure finance candidates lack. Know your edge and lean into it.

Geographic diversification in hiring is accelerating. Dubai, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Abu Dhabi are emerging as secondary hiring hubs. Funds opening offices in these locations are hiring locally and conducting interviews with a mix of local market knowledge questions and global strategy discussions. If you are targeting a non-US office, expect questions about local market structure, regulatory environment, and cross-border investment considerations.


## Patterns from Practitioners

The stock pitch remains the gold standard for evaluation, but modeling tests are making a comeback. After several years of declining usage (some funds dropped them entirely, calling them "artificial"), 41.2% of firms in our 2026 dataset include a modeling test. The return is driven by hiring managers who found that strong pitchers sometimes lacked basic modeling discipline.

The modeling test renaissance is concentrated at multi-manager platforms and mid-size funds ($2B-$10B AUM). Larger single-manager funds and the smallest emerging managers are less likely to include them, though for different reasons: large funds trust their extensive interview process to surface modeling ability naturally, while emerging managers often lack the bandwidth to evaluate detailed model output.

When modeling tests appear, the format has evolved. Rather than "build a model from scratch in 3 hours," some firms now provide a partially completed model with intentional errors and ask the candidate to identify problems, fix them, and extend the analysis. This tests debugging ability and attention to detail, which are more relevant to the actual job than building from a blank spreadsheet.

Take-home case studies are getting longer and more detailed. The average case study window has expanded from 2-3 days to 4-7 days at competitive funds. Some firms now provide proprietary data sets, expert interview transcripts, or management meeting notes as part of the case study materials. This allows candidates to demonstrate research synthesis skills, not just financial modeling.

The quality bar for case study deliverables has risen substantially. Funds report that the top 10.0% of submissions now include alternative data analysis, primary research notes, and scenario frameworks that go well beyond a traditional DCF and recommendation. Candidates who produce investment committee-quality memos stand out immediately.

Multi-manager platforms focus more heavily on risk management and position sizing in interviews. Questions like "How do you determine your initial position size?" and "Walk me through your stop-loss framework" and "How do you think about gross and net exposure at the portfolio level?" are standard at Citadel, Millennium, and similar platforms. This reflects the multi-manager model's emphasis on risk-adjusted returns over absolute performance.

Single-manager funds care more about long-term thesis development. Questions at these firms tend toward: "Tell me about an idea you have followed for 2+ years" and "How does your view on this company differ from when you first started covering it?" and "What would make you change your mind on your highest-conviction position?" The emphasis is on patience and intellectual evolution, not quick trades.

ESG and responsible investing questions are appearing more frequently. This trend is uneven across the industry. Long-only managers and European-based funds are more likely to ask about ESG frameworks. Multi-manager platforms and US-based long/short funds are less likely, though 14.7% of their interview processes now include at least one ESG-related question, up from 6.0% in 2023. Even at firms that do not invest with an explicit ESG mandate, interviewers want to know if you consider ESG factors as part of your risk assessment.


## The "No Case Study" Trend

A growing minority of firms (approximately 18.0% in our dataset) have eliminated formal case studies and modeling tests entirely. Instead, they conduct extended market discussions in real time.

The format varies. Some firms bring a candidate in for a 2-hour session where they discuss 5-6 stocks or themes in rapid succession. Others give the candidate 30 minutes of preparation with a Bloomberg terminal and then hold a 60-minute discussion about what they found.

Proponents of this approach argue that it better simulates the actual job. An analyst does not spend 5 days writing a polished memo in isolation. They synthesize information quickly, form views, and communicate them to the PM in real time.

Critics argue that it favors candidates who are already covering the relevant sectors and disadvantages career changers or candidates from different investment backgrounds.

Either way, this trend means you should be prepared for both formats. Having a few well-rehearsed stock pitches is not enough. You need to be able to discuss markets broadly, react to new information on the fly, and form opinions quickly with incomplete data.


## Framework Fatigue

Some hiring managers have expressed explicit frustration with candidates who default to rigid analytical frameworks. "If I hear one more candidate walk me through a DCF with a terminal growth rate of 3.0% and a WACC of 10.0%, I am going to scream" is a paraphrased quote from a PM at a $5B long/short fund.

The pushback is not against DCF as a methodology. It is against candidates who treat it as the only answer and apply it mechanically without adjusting for the specific situation. A DCF makes sense for a stable-growth utility. It is largely irrelevant for a pre-revenue biotech or a cyclical commodity producer.

What hiring managers want instead:

Flexible valuation thinking. Can you move between absolute and relative valuation depending on the situation? Do you know when to use EV/EBITDA versus P/E versus price-to-book versus private market transaction multiples?

Creative variant perception. The best interview answers come from candidates who have done primary research that sell-side analysts have not done. Channel checks, industry expert calls, patent analysis, supply chain mapping, customer interviews. These signal a research edge that no framework can replicate.

Real-time analytical agility. Given a new piece of information mid-interview (an earnings surprise, a regulatory change, a competitor announcement), can you update your thesis in real time and articulate how it changes your view? This tests whether you think in live markets or in static models.

Scenario thinking over point estimates. Rather than "my price target is $150," strong candidates say "in my base case, the stock is worth $140-$160. In my bear case, if their new product launch fails and margins compress 300 basis points, the downside is $95. The risk-reward skews positive because the bear case is already partially priced in at current levels."


## What This Means for Your Preparation

Build technical depth but do not stop there. Being able to build a three-statement model quickly and accurately is necessary but not sufficient. Layer on alternative data skills, basic programming, and primary research capabilities.

Practice real-time market discussions. Have a view on 10-15 companies across 2-3 sectors. Be able to discuss them without notes, including recent developments, key metrics, and your assessment of the investment opportunity.

Develop your risk management vocabulary. Understand position sizing, stop losses, gross and net exposure, factor exposure, correlation risk, and drawdown management. Even if you are applying for an analyst seat, speaking the language of portfolio management signals PM potential.

Stay current on AI in investing. You do not need to be a machine learning engineer. But you should be able to articulate how LLMs can process large volumes of text data, how satellite imagery provides alternative signals, and how systematic screening can complement your fundamental process. "I use ChatGPT to summarize 50 earnings transcripts in an hour so I can focus my deep research on the 3-4 companies with the most interesting divergence from consensus" is a practical and impressive answer.

Know the fund you are interviewing with. Read their most recent investor letter if available. Review their 13F filings to understand portfolio positioning. Search for interviews with the CIO or senior PMs. Reference specific, informed observations about their strategy during your interview. This level of preparation is rare and consistently cited by hiring managers as a differentiator.


## Compensation Trends Worth Watching

The guaranteed-year structure is evolving. Historically, first-year guarantees were standard for lateral hires. In the current market, some funds are extending guarantees to 2 years and including minimum bonus floors that exceed what was typical even 3 years ago. This reflects intense competition for proven analysts.

Sign-on bonuses have increased 25.0-30.0% at the platform level since 2023. For senior analysts moving between multi-manager platforms, sign-on packages of $300K-$1M are not uncommon, especially when the candidate is leaving unvested deferred compensation at their current fund.

Deferred compensation structures are becoming more standardized but also more complex. Some funds now offer tiered deferral rates that increase with seniority and total compensation. Others have introduced co-investment vehicles that allow analysts to invest alongside the fund at reduced fees, effectively increasing the total compensation package without increasing the fund's cash outflow.

Equity participation and profit-sharing arrangements at smaller funds are gaining traction as a recruiting tool. Emerging managers who cannot compete with platform-level cash compensation are offering meaningful economics (0.5-2.0% of fund profits) to attract senior talent. This creates a different risk-reward calculation for candidates: lower guaranteed compensation but potentially uncapped upside if the fund performs.

Remote and hybrid work arrangements remain limited in hedge funds compared to other industries. Approximately 82.0% of funds require 4-5 days per week in office. The few that offer meaningful flexibility tend to be quantitative or systematic funds where the work is more independent. For fundamental equity roles, the collaborative nature of investment discussions keeps most teams in-person.


## What Candidates Should Track

Monitor 13F filings quarterly. Every hedge fund with over $100M in long US equity positions must disclose quarterly. This data tells you what funds are buying, selling, and holding. Before any interview, review the fund's most recent 13F and be prepared to discuss specific positions.

Follow hedge fund performance data through sources like Hedge Fund Research (HFR), eVestment, and PivotalPath. Understanding which strategies are performing well helps you target your search toward funds that are likely to be hiring and paying well.

Track headhunter activity. When a specific recruiter suddenly has 5 new mandates at a particular fund, it often signals expansion or turnover. Both create opportunity. Stay connected with 2-3 recruiters and check in quarterly even when you are not actively looking.

Read investor letters when they are publicly available or leaked. Letters from firms like Pershing Square, Third Point, Greenlight Capital, and Baupost provide insight into how top investors think and communicate. This shapes your own communication style and gives you material to reference in interviews.

Pay attention to fund launches and spin-outs. When a senior PM leaves a platform to start their own fund, they typically hire 3-8 analysts in the first year. These early hires often receive more responsibility, better economics, and faster career progression than they would at an established firm. The trade-off is higher risk: if the fund does not raise enough capital or underperforms, you may be looking again in 18 months.
