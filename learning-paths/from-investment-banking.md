# From Investment Banking to Hedge Fund

IB to hedge fund is the most well-traveled path on the buy side.
Roughly 40.0% of junior hedge fund analysts come from investment banking backgrounds.
The transition works because you already have the technical foundation and the work ethic.
What you need is a fundamentally different way of thinking about companies.

In banking, you execute. On the buy side, you decide.


## What You Already Have

Your banking background gives you a real head start.
Here is what transfers directly.

Financial modeling is the big one.
You can build a 3-statement model, run a DCF, pull together comps and precedent transactions.
Most candidates from other backgrounds spend months learning what you already do every day.

Valuation methodology is second nature to you.
DCF, trading comps, precedent transactions, sum-of-the-parts.
You understand when each approach applies and where each one breaks down.

Excel mastery matters more than people admit.
Speed in a spreadsheet translates directly to speed in building investment models.
You are not fumbling with VLOOKUP when you should be thinking about the business.

Deal execution taught you how transactions actually work.
M&A mechanics, capital raises, restructurings.
This gives you context that pure research analysts lack.

Client management gave you experience presenting to senior professionals and handling pushback on your analysis.
That skill transfers directly to pitching ideas to a portfolio manager.

Work ethic is not glamorous but it matters.
You already know what 80-hour weeks feel like.
The buy side is demanding in different ways, but you will not be shocked by the intensity.

Understanding of capital markets rounds out the picture.
You know how debt and equity markets function, how pricing works, how deals get done.
This is background knowledge that takes others years to develop.


## What You Need to Learn

The gap between banking and the buy side is not technical.
It is philosophical.

Investment thinking is the biggest shift.
In banking, someone else decides what to buy.
You build the model, you run the analysis, you make the book look good.
On the buy side, you generate the ideas.
You need to develop a variant perception, a view on a company that differs from what the market already believes.
Without that, you have nothing.

Thesis development goes deeper than a banking recommendation.
You need to articulate why you think the stock is mispriced, what the market is missing, and what catalysts will close the gap between your view and the current price.
A good thesis has 3 to 5 key drivers and a clear timeframe.

Portfolio context changes everything about how you think about a position.
In banking, each deal stands alone.
On the buy side, every position exists within a portfolio.
You need to understand position sizing, how much capital to allocate based on conviction and risk.
You need to think about correlation, how your positions relate to each other.
You need to understand risk management at the portfolio level, not just the single-stock level.

Independent research is a real adjustment.
In banking, you have a team.
Analysts, associates, VPs all contribute to the work product.
On the buy side, especially at smaller funds, you are doing your own channel checks.
You are calling customers, suppliers, competitors.
You are reading 10-Ks line by line without anyone telling you which sections matter.

Market awareness means you need to follow markets in real time.
Not just checking the S&P at lunch.
You need to understand macro sensitivity: how interest rate moves affect your positions, how currency shifts change the earnings picture, how sector rotation impacts your book.
This is a daily practice, not an occasional check-in.


## Why This Transition Works

You have the best technical foundation of any candidate pool.
When a PM asks you to model a scenario, you do not need training.
You already speak the language of finance, which means networking conversations are productive from day one.
You understand how companies raise capital, how they think about their balance sheet, and how transactions create or destroy value.

The learning curve is steep but narrow.
You are adding investment judgment on top of a solid technical base, rather than building everything from scratch.


## Suggested Path: 2 to 3 Months

This is an aggressive timeline, but IB analysts are used to aggressive timelines.

Step 1: Start generating investment ideas from day one.
Do not wait until you feel ready.
Pick 3 companies you know from deal work and write down why you would buy or sell each one.
These first attempts will be rough.
That is the point.
You learn investment thinking by doing it, not by reading about it.

Step 2: Build stock pitch presentations.
Take your best idea and build a full pitch deck.
Thesis, variant perception, financial model, catalysts, risks, valuation.
You already know how to make a polished deck.
Now fill it with your own thinking instead of a client's.

Step 3: Learn portfolio construction basics.
Read about position sizing, Kelly criterion, risk budgeting.
Understand how a portfolio manager thinks about adding a new position.
How does it interact with existing holdings?
What is the right size given your conviction level and the risk profile?

Step 4: Practice with mock pitches.
Find a friend on the buy side or use interview prep services.
Pitch your idea cold.
The first few times will be humbling.
Interviewers will ask questions you never considered.
That feedback is invaluable.

Step 5: Network with buy-side professionals.
Use your banking network.
Every deal you worked on involved a buy-side counterparty.
Reach out to the analysts and PMs you interacted with during transactions.
They understand your background and can give you direct feedback on your readiness.

Step 6: Apply to on-cycle and off-cycle recruiting.
On-cycle happens earlier every year, sometimes 12 to 18 months before start dates.
Off-cycle is ongoing and often less competitive.
Cast a wide net across fund sizes and strategies.


## Common Pitfalls for IB Candidates

Treating the stock pitch like a sell-side initiating coverage report.
Your pitch needs a variant perception, not just a summary of the business.

Over-relying on DCF.
Hedge fund managers care about multiple valuation approaches and want to know which one matters most for this specific company.

Not having a market view.
You will be asked what you think about the market environment.
Have an opinion and be able to defend it.

Talking about deals instead of investments.
Your interviewer does not care about the M&A transaction you closed.
They care about whether you can identify a mispriced security.

Underselling your edge.
You have real exposure to how companies think about capital allocation.
Use that insight in your investment thesis.
It is something most candidates cannot offer.
