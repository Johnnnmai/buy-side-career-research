# Financial Modeling and Valuation

Financial modeling in a hedge fund interview is fundamentally different from investment banking modeling. In banking, the model is the deliverable. In hedge fund interviews, the model is a tool to support an investment thesis. Interviewers care less about whether your balance sheet balances to the penny and more about whether you understand what drives the business and how those drivers translate to valuation.

That said, you need to be technically proficient. If you cannot build a 3-statement model from a 10-K in two to three hours, you are not ready for hedge fund interviews. Technical competence is table stakes -- what separates you is how you think about the model's outputs.


## What interviewers evaluate

Technical accuracy. Can you build a model that works? Do the three statements link correctly? Does the cash flow statement reconcile? Are your formulas correct? Errors signal carelessness, which is disqualifying when the job involves managing real capital.

Speed. Hedge fund modeling tests are timed. A 3-statement model build from a 10-K typically gets two to three hours. An LBO model gets one to two hours. If you need four hours to build a basic model, you are too slow. Speed comes from repetition -- there is no shortcut.

Understanding of drivers. This is where hedge fund modeling diverges from banking. Interviewers want to see that you understand what actually drives the business. Can you identify the two or three key assumptions that matter most? If you are modeling a retailer, same-store sales growth and new unit openings matter far more than the depreciation schedule.

Sensitivity to key assumptions. Can you quickly articulate how your valuation changes if your key assumptions are wrong? If revenue growth comes in at 6.0% instead of 10.0%, what happens to your target price? This is where a model becomes an investment tool rather than an academic exercise.

Intellectual honesty about precision. The best candidates acknowledge that a model is a framework for thinking, not a crystal ball. Saying "my DCF gives me a price target of $47.32" implies false precision. Saying "my analysis suggests the stock is worth $42 to $52 depending on margin trajectory, with a base case around $47" demonstrates maturity.


## Types of modeling tests

3-statement model build. You receive a company's 10-K and have two to three hours to build a fully linked income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement with projections. This is the most common modeling test at fundamental equity funds.

LBO model. You model a leveraged buyout with multiple tranches of debt, management incentives, and exit scenarios. Common at funds that invest in event-driven or credit situations. You typically get one to two hours.

DCF from scratch. Build a discounted cash flow analysis including explicit forecast period, terminal value, and WACC calculation. Often combined with a 3-statement model as the valuation output.

Comps analysis. Select comparable companies, calculate relevant trading multiples, and apply them to the target company. Interviewers test whether you understand why multiples differ across companies -- growth rates, margins, returns on capital, and balance sheet risk.

Quick mental math valuation. "This company earns $3 per share and trades at $45. It grows earnings 10.0% per year. Is it cheap?" These quick questions test whether you can do rough valuation math in your head during a conversation.


## Sample questions

"Build a 3-statement model for this company using their 10-K."

"Walk me through a DCF. What is the most important assumption?"

"How do you think about terminal value?"

"When would you use EV/EBITDA versus P/E?"

"This company trades at 15x earnings. Is it cheap or expensive?"

"How does a $10M asset write-down flow through the three statements?"

"What is the difference between levered and unlevered free cash flow?"

"Walk me through an LBO model and what drives returns."

"If EBITDA margins expand by 200 basis points, what is the impact on free cash flow?"

"How do you calculate WACC? What discount rate would you use for this company?"

"Why might two companies in the same industry trade at different multiples?"

"What is the difference between enterprise value and equity value?"

"When does a DCF give you a higher value than a comparables analysis?"

"How do you project working capital for a company with seasonal revenue?"

"Walk me through how you would model a company that is transitioning from hardware to subscription revenue."

"What is the right terminal growth rate for this business and why?"

"If this company issues $500M of debt to buy back stock, what happens to EPS? What happens to the stock price?"

"How do you think about normalized earnings versus reported earnings?"

"What adjustments would you make to this company's reported EBITDA?"

"A company trades at 8x EV/EBITDA. The peer group trades at 12x. What could explain the discount?"


## Key concepts to master

Revenue build. Break revenue into its component drivers. For a retailer, that is store count times average revenue per store. For a SaaS company, that is customer count times average contract value times net retention rate. For a bank, that is net interest income plus fee income. The revenue build should reflect how the business actually generates revenue.

Margin analysis. Understand the difference between gross margin, EBITDA margin, and operating margin. Know what drives each. Can the company expand margins through operating leverage, pricing power, or mix shift? Or are margins likely to compress due to competition, rising input costs, or investment spending?

Working capital dynamics. Understand how changes in accounts receivable, inventory, and accounts payable affect cash flow. A company can be profitable and still run out of cash if working capital is growing faster than revenue.

Capital expenditure analysis. Distinguish between maintenance capex (required to sustain the business) and growth capex (invested to grow the business). This distinction matters for free cash flow calculation and valuation.

Return on invested capital. ROIC is the single most important metric for evaluating business quality. A company that earns 25.0% ROIC and reinvests at that rate is fundamentally more valuable than one earning 8.0% ROIC. Understand how to calculate ROIC and what drives it.


## Common mistakes

Memorizing formulas without understanding drivers. Knowing that unlevered free cash flow equals EBIT times one minus tax rate plus depreciation minus capex minus change in working capital is not enough. You need to understand why each component matters and what drives it for the specific company you are modeling.

Over-building the model. A 15-tab model with granular assumptions on every line item is not better than a focused model that nails the three key drivers. In a timed test, prioritize the assumptions that actually move the valuation.

Anchoring to the current stock price. Do not reverse-engineer your assumptions to hit the current stock price. Build your model based on your fundamental view and let the output tell you whether the stock is cheap or expensive.

Ignoring the balance sheet. Many candidates focus on the income statement and treat the balance sheet as an afterthought. The balance sheet matters -- leverage, liquidity, upcoming maturities, and capital structure all affect equity value.

False precision. Projecting revenue to four decimal places or terminal value to the exact dollar suggests you do not understand the inherent uncertainty in financial projections. Use round numbers and ranges.


## Preparation

Practice building models from scratch with a timer. Download a 10-K for a company you have not analyzed before and give yourself three hours to build a 3-statement model with projections and a valuation. Do this at least five times before your interviews.

Study business models before studying accounting. Understanding how a company makes money is more important than memorizing the accounting treatment for operating leases. Start with the business model and let the accounting follow.

Practice mental math. You should be able to quickly calculate earnings yields, growth-adjusted multiples, and back-of-the-envelope valuations during a conversation. This comes from practice.
