# Portfolio Project Ideas

(draft)

These are project ideas that demonstrate hedge fund analytical skills relevant to buy-side interviews.
A strong portfolio is the single best thing you can do to stand out as a candidate.
Your resume gets you in the door.
Your portfolio gets you the offer.

Every project here is designed to show a hiring manager that you think like an investor, not just someone who wants to work at a hedge fund.


## 1. Stock Pitch Memo: Long Idea

Build a full investment memo for a long position.
This is the foundational project and the one you will use most in interviews.

Your memo should include a clear thesis with a variant perception, a financial model with explicit assumptions, 2 to 3 catalysts with specific timelines, a risk section that honestly addresses what could go wrong, and a valuation that supports your thesis.

A good long pitch answers one question: why is the market wrong about this company, and what will cause the market to correct its mistake?

Length: 4 to 6 pages plus a supporting financial model.

Skills demonstrated: fundamental analysis, financial modeling, thesis development, written communication.

Complexity: beginner to intermediate.
This is where everyone should start.

Tips: Pick a company you genuinely find interesting.
Your enthusiasm will come through in the analysis.
Avoid mega-caps where you cannot realistically have a variant view.
Mid-caps with $2B to $20B market caps are the sweet spot for demonstrating original thinking.


## 2. Short Thesis: Company Deterioration

Identify a business that is deteriorating and build a case for why the stock should go down.
Short theses are harder to construct and rarer among candidates, which makes them more valuable.

Your analysis should cover the signs of deterioration: declining margins, market share loss, management turnover, accounting red flags, or secular headwinds.
You need specific catalysts that will force the market to recognize the deterioration.
And you need to address the bear case against your short: what happens if management executes a turnaround?

Skills demonstrated: forensic accounting, identifying red flags, short thesis construction, contrarian thinking.

Complexity: intermediate.
Requires deeper analytical skills than a long pitch because the burden of proof is higher for a short.

Tips: Focus on business fundamentals, not valuation alone.
"The stock is expensive" is not a short thesis.
"The company is losing market share to a better product and the market has not yet reflected this in estimates" is a short thesis.
Look at companies with declining returns on invested capital, rising customer churn, or management teams that are selling stock.


## 3. Sector Deep Dive

Build a comprehensive analysis of an industry sector.
This project demonstrates your ability to think about competitive dynamics, value chains, and where the investment opportunities sit within an ecosystem.

Cover the industry structure: who the key players are, how the value chain works, what drives profitability at each stage.
Map the competitive dynamics: who is gaining share, who is losing, what are the barriers to entry.
Identify the key drivers: what macro and micro factors determine sector performance?
End with your top picks: 2 to 3 names you would own within the sector, with brief investment theses for each.

Skills demonstrated: industry analysis, competitive mapping, comparative valuation, strategic thinking.

Complexity: intermediate to advanced.
Requires broad research across multiple companies and a strong understanding of industry economics.

Tips: Choose a sector where you have some natural advantage, whether through work experience, personal interest, or academic background.
A healthcare consultant should write about healthcare.
A consumer-focused MBA should write about retail or consumer brands.
The sector deep dive is where domain expertise shines.


## 4. Event-Driven Case Study

Analyze a specific corporate event: a merger, spin-off, restructuring, or activist campaign.
Event-driven investing is a distinct strategy, and this project shows that you can evaluate situations where the catalyst is defined and the timeline is bounded.

Cover the event itself: what is happening, who is involved, what are the terms.
Analyze the risk-reward: what is the upside if the event completes as expected, what is the downside if it breaks, what is the probability of each outcome.
Build a timeline of key milestones: regulatory approvals, shareholder votes, closing dates.
Identify what the market is pricing and where you disagree.

Skills demonstrated: deal analysis, risk/reward assessment, timeline analysis, probability-weighted valuation.

Complexity: advanced.
Requires understanding of corporate transactions, regulatory processes, and risk-arbitrage math.

Tips: Use a current or recent event so your analysis is fresh and you can discuss the real-time dynamics.
Avoid ancient case studies unless you are explicitly doing a post-mortem exercise.
Show that you understand the mechanics of the transaction, not just the strategic rationale.


## 5. Portfolio Construction Exercise

Build a hypothetical 10 to 15 position portfolio with explicit risk constraints.
This project demonstrates that you think at the portfolio level, not just at the single-stock level.

Start with a clear investment mandate: what is your strategy, what is your benchmark, what are your constraints?
Construct a portfolio with explicit position sizing rationale.
Show how positions interact: what are the correlations, where are the concentrations, what are the factor exposures?
Include a risk budget: how much tracking error are you willing to take, how do you manage sector exposure, what is your gross and net exposure?

Skills demonstrated: portfolio construction, position sizing, correlation analysis, risk budgeting.

Complexity: advanced.
Requires understanding of portfolio theory and practical risk management.

Tips: Keep it realistic.
A 15-position long/short portfolio with 120.0% gross exposure and 40.0% net exposure is more credible than a 50-position portfolio that you clearly have not researched deeply.
Show your work on position sizing.
Explain why your highest-conviction position is 8.0% and your lowest is 2.0%.


## 6. Earnings Model and Variant Analysis

Build a detailed earnings model for a company where your estimates differ materially from the Street consensus.
Then explain why you think the Street is wrong.

Your model should be built from the revenue drivers up, not from a top-line growth assumption.
Break out revenue by segment, geography, or product line.
Model the cost structure with explicit assumptions about operating leverage.
Show how your estimates compare to consensus at every level: revenue, gross profit, EBITDA, EPS.

The variant analysis is the key.
For each line where you differ from consensus, explain your reasoning.
Are you using proprietary data?
Different assumptions about market growth?
A different view on competitive dynamics?
The quality of your variant analysis determines the quality of the project.

Skills demonstrated: financial modeling, channel checks, variant perception, analytical conviction.

Complexity: intermediate.
The modeling is straightforward, but developing a genuine variant view requires real research.

Tips: Pick a company that is about to report earnings so you can test your thesis in real time.
Nothing is more impressive in an interview than saying "I built this model before earnings, predicted the miss on X line, and the stock reacted exactly as I expected."


## 7. Historical Investment Post-Mortem

Analyze a famous investment and extract lessons.
What went right, what went wrong, and what you would have done differently with the information available at the time.

Good candidates for post-mortem analysis:

Bill Ackman and Herbalife: conviction versus thesis risk.
John Paulson and subprime: identifying systemic risk before the market.
David Einhorn and Lehman Brothers: forensic analysis under institutional pressure.
Carl Icahn and Apple: activist investing at mega-cap scale.
Michael Burry and GameStop: value investing meets market structure.

Cover the thesis as it existed at the time, not with hindsight.
Analyze the decision-making process.
Identify what the investor got right and what they missed.
Draw specific lessons that apply to your own investment process.

Skills demonstrated: analytical thinking, understanding of investment process, ability to learn from others, historical market knowledge.

Complexity: beginner.
This is the most accessible project because the research materials are publicly available.
But a thoughtful post-mortem demonstrates intellectual maturity.

Tips: Avoid just retelling the story.
Every finance student knows the basics of Paulson and the subprime trade.
Add your own analytical layer.
What would you have done at each decision point?
How would you have sized the position?
When would you have exited?


## Interview-Specific Portfolio Tips

Publish your work somewhere accessible.
A Substack, a personal website, or even a well-formatted Google Doc that you can share via link.
Interviewers who can read your work before the meeting will ask better questions, and the conversation will be more productive for both sides.

Include your financial models as downloadable Excel files.
Interviewers will want to click through your assumptions, stress-test your scenarios, and see how you built the model.
A pitch without a model is an opinion without evidence.

Consider recording Loom walkthroughs of your key pitches.
A 10-minute video walkthrough of your best stock pitch shows communication skills, conviction, and depth of understanding.
Some candidates include these links in their resume or cover letter.

Keep your portfolio updated.
A stock pitch from 18 months ago that you have not revisited signals a lack of genuine interest.
Update your thesis as new information emerges.
Track your performance honestly, including your misses.


## How to Pick a Project

Start with a sector you care about.
Genuine interest produces better analysis because you will naturally spend more time thinking about the companies.

Find a company with a story.
The best pitches are about companies going through some kind of change: a new product cycle, a management transition, a market share shift, a restructuring.
Change creates mispricing.
Companies in steady state are harder to pitch because there is less room for variant perception.

Build the analysis iteratively.
Read the 10-K, build the model, listen to the earnings calls, do channel checks, and let your thesis evolve as you learn more.
The best stock pitches are built over weeks, not over a weekend.

Test your thesis against smart people.
Share your work with friends in finance, post it on investment forums, or discuss it in interview prep groups.
If your thesis cannot survive a conversation, it will not survive an interview.


## What Hiring Managers Actually Look At

Conviction.
Do you have a clear point of view?
Do you defend it without being rigid?
Can you distinguish between your thesis being wrong and the market needing more time?

Analytical rigor.
Is your model thoughtful?
Are your assumptions explicit and reasonable?
Did you actually do the work or did you just copy a sell-side report?

Risk awareness.
Do you know what could go wrong?
Have you quantified the downside?
Can you articulate when you would admit you were wrong?

Communication clarity.
Can you explain your thesis in 2 minutes?
Is your written memo structured and easy to follow?
Do you get to the point or do you bury the thesis under background?

Intellectual curiosity.
Do you follow up on your ideas?
Do you update your thesis?
Do you read widely?
The best analysts are obsessively curious about how businesses work.

Process.
Do you have a repeatable approach to generating and evaluating ideas?
Or is every pitch a one-off effort?
Managers want to hire someone who will generate a steady flow of ideas, not just one good pitch.


## Writing a Good Investment Memo

Start with the thesis in the first paragraph.
Do not make the reader wait 3 pages to understand your point of view.
State the stock, the direction (long or short), the variant perception, the expected return, and the time horizon in the first 5 sentences.

Structure the rest logically: business overview, industry context, thesis drivers (3 to 5 key points), financial analysis and valuation, risks and mitigants, catalysts and timeline.

Be specific.
"The company has a strong competitive position" is vague.
"The company has 42.0% market share in a market where the number 2 player has 18.0%, and switching costs are high due to 3-year contracts" is specific.

Show your work.
Include the model, the data sources, the channel checks.
The memo is the summary.
The supporting materials are the evidence.

End with a clear recommendation: buy at current price of X with a target of Y over Z months, implying N% upside against a downside case of W, implying M% risk.
